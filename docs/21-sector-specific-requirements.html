Chapter 21: Sector-Specific Requirements

Part VI: Regulation & Compliance

In this chapter

  • How financial services pair model risk management (Fed SR 11-7 → SR 26-2), operational resilience (EU DORA), credit explainability (CFPB), and Indian supervision (RBI FREE-AI, SEBI).
  • How healthcare and life sciences separate non-device CDS from regulated SaMD, authorize model updates via FDA PCCPs, protect PHI under HIPAA, and unify EU MDR and EU AI Act compliance (MDCG 2025-6).
  • How employment and HR deployments trigger European works-council co-determination (BetrVG § 87(1)(6)), EU AI Act bans and notices, the Platform Work Directive, and U.S. NYC LL 144 / Illinois HB 3773 audits.
  • How public-sector and critical-infrastructure AI activates OMB M-25-22 acquisition safeguards, Canada DADM impact levels, EU NIS2 / CER incident clocks, and DHS/CISA OT protections.

Horizontal AI statutes set only the baseline. Chapter 20 mapped country-level privacy and AI laws across major jurisdictions. Yet even where no omnibus AI statute applies—or where an EU deadline sits out to 2027 or 2028—vertical supervisors already inspect, sanction, and block production AI today. Banking examiners scrutinize undocumented credit models; medical-device agencies seize unapproved clinical triage software; European works councils obtain injunctions against copilot telemetry; and energy or government buyers reject AI vendors lacking operational-resilience and lock-in clauses. Compliance teams must understand both the horizontal rulebook and the sharper vertical teeth underneath it.

Illustrative Composite Scenario: Four Verticals, Four Hard Stops #

A diversified multinational industrial and services group greenlights a unified generative-AI platform across four operating divisions after clearing generic corporate InfoSec review. Within sixty days, four vertical regulators halt rollouts independently:

  • Financial Services: A lending subsidiary deploys an LLM credit-memo and anomaly-triage assistant built on a single cloud API missing EU DORA exit/audit clauses and Article 28 Register of Information entries; bank examiners simultaneously demand materiality-tiered challenge documentation aligned with Fed SR 26-2 and RBI FREE-AI.
  • Healthcare & MedTech: A diagnostics unit embeds generative summarization that recommends diagnostic follow-ups without a HIPAA BAA, § 164.514 de-identification, or FDA SaMD / PCCP authorization, while German hospitals refuse procurement without unified EU MDR + MDCG 2025-6 technical files.
  • HR & Workplace IT: European IT activates corporate enterprise copilot productivity tracking in Hamburg without consulting the works council under BetrVG § 87(1)(6), while recruiting turns on facial emotion scoring (EU AI Act Art. 5(1)(f) banned) alongside un-audited screening in New York and Illinois.
  • Energy & Public Sector: A grid-forecasting bid to a European utility and a U.S. federal agency collapses when procurement finds zero EU NIS2 24h/72h cyber reporting hooks, missing OMB M-25-22 data-rights protections, and no Canada DADM impact-level disclosures.

(This composite scenario is illustrative; it is not drawn from a specific organization or incident.)

21.1 Financial Services: Model Risk Management, EU DORA, and Prudential Oversight #

Financial institutions pioneered quantitative AI oversight more than a decade before generative AI arrived. Rather than waiting for omnibus AI statutes, banking and securities supervisors govern AI simultaneously through model risk management, ICT operational resilience, fair-lending transparency, and market-conduct audits.

From SR 11-7 to SR 26-2: Effective Challenge Meets Proportionality: For fifteen years, Federal Reserve SR 11-7 / OCC Bulletin 2011-12 defined banking AI governance around an enterprise inventory, three-pillar validation (conceptual soundness, ongoing monitoring, outcomes analysis), and independent "effective challenge" by reviewers with the competence, independence, and authority to block unsafe models.[1] On April 17, 2026, U.S. federal banking agencies issued SR 26-2 / OCC Bulletin 2026-13 / FIL-15-2026, jointly superseding SR 11-7.[2] Crucially, SR 26-2 pivots away from rigid, checkbox annual validation calendars toward materiality-based proportionality—focusing deepest validation rigor on models carrying significant financial, credit, liquidity, or consumer-harm risk. Equally important, SR 26-2 explicitly places general-purpose generative and agentic AI outside narrow statistical MRM scope while expecting banks to govern them through broader enterprise risk management (ERM). In practice, that is an architectural distinction without a free pass: if an LLM scores commercial borrowers, summarizes KYC alerts, or executes treasury steps, examiners still demand documented conceptual soundness, RAG regression testing, vendor due diligence, and independent challenge.

EU Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA): Applicable in full since January 17, 2025, Regulation (EU) 2022/2554 (DORA) treats third-party AI APIs, hosting clouds, and vector platforms as critical ICT infrastructure rather than mere model choices.[3] EU banks, insurers, investment firms, and payment institutions must satisfy four hard operational duties on AI supply chains:

  • Register of Information (Article 28): Every financial entity must maintain and report a standardized contract registry mapping all ICT third-party AI providers, supporting critical/important functions, down through fourth-party sub-processors (exposing silent reliance on one underlying frontier-model API across five fintech SaaS vendors).
  • Mandatory Contract & Exit Clauses: AI vendor agreements supporting critical functions require unrestricted regulator inspection/audit rights, data-location clarity, service-level security targets, and tested termination exit plans.
  • Threat-Led Penetration Testing (TLPT, Articles 26–27): Significant entities must run live red-team exercises spanning production ICT systems supporting critical functions—including exposed AI gateways and agent integrations.
  • Critical ICT Third-Party Provider (CTPP) Oversight: Major cloud and AI infrastructure suppliers designated as CTPPs fall under direct lead supervision by the European Supervisory Authorities (ESAs).

Fair Lending & Market Conduct (CFPB, RBI FREE-AI, SEBI): Outside balance-sheet safety, conduct watchdogs police how models treat borrowers and investors. In the United States, CFPB Circular 2023-03 confirms creditors cannot rely on generic Regulation B checklists when complex AI/ML models reject applicants: lenders must output the specific principal reasons driving each adverse credit decision.[4] In India, the RBI FREE-AI Committee Report (August 13, 2025) sets 26 actionable expectations across banks and NBFCs covering board-approved AI policies, centralized model inventories, red-teaming, vendor governance, and audit assurance.[5] Meanwhile, SEBI (LODR Reg. 21) anchors AI/cyber governance inside Risk Management Committees of top listed entities while integrating AI/ML advisory and trading tools into brokers' mandatory annual system audits—requiring deterministic pre-trade limits and automated kill-switches.[6]

The Guardrails #

  1. Apply SR 26-2 Materiality Tiering + SR 11-7 Effective Challenge: Route revenue, credit, trading, and AML models through independent second-line validation regardless of whether an engine uses regression weights or LLM prompts ([1], [2]).
  2. Map Every AI Dependency in the DORA Register of Information: Track third- and fourth-party model hosts under Article 28, enforce DORA audit/exit addenda, and include critical AI endpoints in TLPT scopes ([3]).
  3. Generate Specific Adverse-Action Reasons & Kill-Switches: Pair lending AI with calibrated feature attributions (SHAP/reason codes) meeting CFPB Circular 2023-03 ([4]), and gate financial/trading bots with deterministic kill-switches aligned with RBI FREE-AI and SEBI audit packs ([5], [6]).

21.2 Healthcare and Life Sciences: HIPAA, Medical Devices, and Clinical Safety #

Healthcare and life sciences AI triggers two strict regulatory gates simultaneously: patient health-information privacy (enforced at ingestion and inference) and medical-device premarket safety (enforced whenever software steps across administrative convenience into clinical decision-making).

Protecting PHI Under HIPAA (45 CFR Parts 160 & 164): Covered entities (hospitals, health plans, clearinghouses) and their technology suppliers handle Protected Health Information (PHI) under strict statutory rules.[7] Pasting clinical notes, diagnostic images, or scheduling records into uncontracted consumer LLM endpoints constitutes an immediate unauthorized disclosure. Enterprise healthcare deployments require:

  • Executed Business Associate Agreements (BAAs, 45 CFR §§ 164.502(e), 164.504(e)): Every foundation-model vendor, vector database host, and observability pipeline touching PHI must sign a BAA alongside technical Zero Data Retention (ZDR) and zero-training terms enforcing the Minimum Necessary standard.
  • Verified De-Identification (45 CFR § 164.514): Training or evaluating models without patient authorization requires formal de-identification under either the Safe Harbor rule (stripping 18 explicit identifier classes with no actual knowledge of re-identifiability) or Expert Determination (statistical proof of very small re-identification risk). Crucially, stripping structured MRNs under Safe Harbor routinely fails on unstructured clinical free text that preserves rare diagnoses, clinician names, or dates—making automated NER scrubbing and statistical review mandatory before data enters clinical corpora.

FDA SaMD, Non-Device CDS, and PCCPs: Under Section 3060 of the 21st Century Cures Act (FD&C Act § 520(o)(1)(E)) and the FDA's September 28, 2022 Clinical Decision Support Software Guidance, software avoids FDA medical-device classification (SaMD) only if it meets four cumulative criteria: it does not analyze medical images or physiological signals; displays medical/patient information; supports recommendations to a healthcare professional rather than replacing clinical judgment; and enables the clinician to independently review the underlying clinical basis without time-critical "black-box" reliance.[8] The moment an ambient clinical scribe or RAG assistant auto-triages emergency referrals, ranks imaging findings, or issues directive diagnostic flags without transparent clinical evidence, it crosses into regulated SaMD requiring 510(k), De Novo, or PMA clearance.

Because post-deployment fine-tuning or continuous retraining previously forced manufacturers back through repeat clearances, FDORA Section 3308 (FD&C Act § 515C) and the FDA's December 2024 final guidance on Predetermined Change Control Plans (PCCPs)—complemented by its January 2025 Lifecycle Management draft guidance—allow device sponsors to pre-authorize bounded model updates inside initial submissions by specifying an exact Modification Protocol, re-training data governance, and validation acceptance thresholds.[8]

EU Dual Conformity (EU MDR 2017/745 + MDCG 2025-6): In Europe, medical AI software falling above Class I under Regulation (EU) 2017/745 (EU MDR) (requiring a Notified Body) automatically enters EU AI Act Annex I High-Risk scope.[9] Clarified by joint guidance MDCG 2025-6 / AIB 2025-1 (June 2025), manufacturers do not create duplicate parallel QMS silos; instead, they expand existing MDR technical documentation and Quality Management Systems to cover AI Act bias/data governance, human oversight, transparency, and cybersecurity—tracked against the August 2, 2028 Annex I compliance date set by Regulation (EU) 2026/1744.[10][11] Across both U.S. and EU regimes, safe clinical deployment requires silent-mode shadow validation, demographic subgroup error checks (catching cost/access proxy bias), and mandatory clinician review before any AI summary enters the medical record.

The Guardrails #

  1. Enforce BAA + ZDR + Dual-Pass § 164.514 De-Identification: Block external model calls unless covered by an executed BAA/ZDR endpoint or audited free-text PHI redaction ([7]).
  2. Gate Feature Creep Against FDA § 520(o)(1)(E) Boundaries: Require Regulatory Affairs sign-off before adding diagnostic triage, severity scoring, or signal/image interpretation to administrative or CDS tools ([8]).
  3. Lock Retraining Pipelines Behind Authorized PCCP Envelopes: Enforce CI/CD release blocks preventing unapproved weight updates on cleared medical devices unless validation passes the authorized PCCP protocol ([8]).
  4. Integrate MDR and AI Act Quality Systems (MDCG 2025-6): Embed EU AI Act data, bias, and human-oversight artifacts directly inside medical-device QMS dossiers with subgroup clinical shadow validation ahead of August 2028 ([9], [10], [11]).

21.3 Employment and HR: Works Councils, Employee Monitoring, and Automated Hiring #

Workplace AI collides with a unique enforcement architecture where labor unions, works councils, municipal civil-rights regulators, and EU safety bans exercise direct veto and injunction powers.

EU High-Risk Status, Prohibitions & Worker Notice: Virtually all AI systems used to recruit, filter, interview, assign tasks, evaluate performance, promote, or terminate workers fall inside EU AI Act Annex III(4) High-Risk.[12] Even ahead of the December 2, 2027 Annex III enforcement date, two critical workplace obligations apply directly: first, Article 5(1)(f) bans emotion recognition systems in the workplace (enforceable since February 2, 2025, eliminating sentiment-inferring webcam/voice interview tools outright outside narrow medical/safety contexts); second, Article 26(7) requires EU deployers to inform workers' representatives and affected workers before putting a high-risk workplace AI system into service.

European Co-Determination (BetrVG § 87(1)(6) & ArbG Hamburg 24 BVGa 1/24): Across Germany, Austria, France (CSE), and the Netherlands, employee works councils possess binding statutory consultation or co-determination veto rights over software capable of monitoring employee behavior or performance. In Germany's landmark Hamburg Labour Court ruling (ArbG Hamburg, January 16, 2024, Case 24 BVGa 1/24), the court held that allowing employees voluntary browser access to external ChatGPT via personal accounts did not trigger co-determination under § 87(1)(1) (workplace order vs. work execution guidelines) or § 87(1)(6) BetrVG because the employer operated no corporate system capturing individual usage telemetry.[13] IT leaders must not misread that narrow ruling as a blanket exemption. The instant an enterprise deploys corporate Copilot licenses, SSO session logging, prompt analytics, keystroke/screen surveillance, or automated productivity dashboards, § 87(1)(6) mandatory co-determination triggers immediately—allowing works councils to halt deployment via court injunction until a formal works agreement (Betriebsvereinbarung) sets clear logging and evaluation boundaries.

Platform Work & Algorithmic Management (Directive (EU) 2024/2831): Reinforcing works-council leverage across Europe, Directive (EU) 2024/2831 (Member State transposition deadline December 2, 2026) establishes binding rules in Articles 6–10 against intrusive automated worker monitoring—explicitly barring emotional-state inference, private-conversation tracking, and off-duty data collection—while requiring transparent parameter disclosures to workers' representatives and guaranteed human review and explanation for impactful automated decisions affecting shifts, pay, or account termination.[14]

U.S. Hiring Audits, Notice & Proxy Bans (NYC LL 144, Illinois HB 3773): Across U.S. recruiting, compliance shifts from labor co-determination to civil-rights transparency and adverse-impact math:

  • NYC Local Law 144: Enforced since July 5, 2023, employers using Automated Employment Decision Tools (AEDTs) for NYC roles must publish annual independent intersectional bias audit summaries on their website careers page and deliver 10-business-day notice with an alternative selection process option.[15]
  • Illinois HB 3773 (775 ILCS 5/2-102(L), effective January 1, 2026): Prohibits discriminatory AI across recruitment, hiring, promotion, discipline, and discharge, mandates employee/applicant notice, and explicitly outlaws using ZIP codes as proxies for protected traits, complementing federal EEOC Title VII and ADA enforcement.[16]

The Guardrails #

  1. Negotiate Works-Council Agreements & Disable Performance Telemetry Before Rollout: Engage German/EU works councils early under BetrVG § 87(1)(6) before enabling enterprise copilots, disabling user-level productivity scoring by default ([13], [14]).
  2. Hard-Ban Emotion Recognition in Recruiting & Coaching: Strip facial affect, tone-stress, and biometric emotion features from all HR and call-center tools to comply with EU AI Act Art. 5(1)(f) and Platform Work principles ([12], [14]).
  3. Standardize Annual Hiring Audits, Proxy Exclusions & Notices: Run annual intersectional four-fifths audits (NYC LL 144), strip ZIP/geographic proxy features (Illinois HB 3773), and deliver unified pre-screening notices with human review paths ([15], [16]).

21.4 Public Sector and Critical Infrastructure #

When AI enters public administration or physical infrastructure—power grids, water utilities, transport networks, telecommunications, emergency dispatch, or benefit determination—regulators shift focus from consumer choice to constitutional due process, sovereign vendor independence, and cyber-physical resilience.

Public-Sector Procurement, Algorithmic Impact & Citizen Due Process:

  • U.S. Federal Governance & Acquisition (OMB M-25-21 & OMB M-25-22): Issued together on April 3, 2025, OMB Memorandum M-25-21 retains federal civilian CAIOs and executive AI Governance Boards over high-impact government use cases,[17] while companion OMB Memorandum M-25-22 (Driving Efficient Acquisition of Artificial Intelligence in Government) reshapes public-sector vendor contracts.[18] Agencies procuring commercial AI must negotiate government data and IP protections, contractual anti-lock-in portability, competitive U.S.-aligned interoperability, and measurable lifecycle risk controls—meaning commercial vendors bidding on federal contracts must expose clean export interfaces and waive proprietary claims over agency training/evaluation telemetry.
  • Canada Directive on Automated Decision-Making (DADM): Effective since April 1, 2019, the Treasury Board's DADM remains one of the clearest operational public-sector accountability blueprints globally.[19] Departments using automated systems for administrative decisions must complete and publicly post an Algorithmic Impact Assessment (AIA) classifying systems across Impact Levels I–IV. Higher levels trigger mandatory independent peer review, public plain-language notices, source-code/documentation access, meaningful individual explanations, and final human decision ownership.
  • EU Public Authorities (EU AI Act Arts. 27 & 49): European public bodies deploying Annex III systems (social benefits, immigration, law enforcement, justice) must perform pre-deployment Fundamental Rights Impact Assessments (FRIAs) and register deployments in the public EU AI database, backing administrative accountability with contestation rights (Article 86).

Critical Infrastructure Cyber-Physical Resilience (NIS2, CER, & DHS/CISA): AI operating inside utilities, telecom backbones, logistics hubs, and financial clearing faces converged operational mandates:

  • EU NIS2 (Directive (EU) 2022/2555) & CER (Directive (EU) 2022/2557): Following its October 17, 2024 transposition deadline, NIS2 subjects essential and important entities across energy, transport, health, water, banking, and digital infrastructure to binding board-accountable cyber governance, supply-chain risk reviews, and aggressive Article 23 incident reporting clocks (24-hour early warning, 72-hour incident assessment, 1-month final report)—reinforced by physical-continuity duties under CER and EU AI Act Annex III(2) high-risk controls where AI acts as a safety component in critical infrastructure.[20]
  • U.S. DHS / CISA Roles and Responsibilities Framework for AI in Critical Infrastructure (November 2024): Classifies operational responsibility across cloud/compute hosts, AI developers, and critical-infrastructure operators along three structural threat vectors—attacks using AI, attacks targeting AI systems (poisoning, adversarial evasion), and design/implementation failures—calling for air-gapped operational technology (OT) boundaries, deterministic safety interlocks, and verified manual fallbacks.[21]

The Guardrails #

  1. Build M-25-22 Portability & DADM/FRIA Transparency into Public Bids: Standardize data-rights preservation, open export schemas, public impact summaries, and human-appeal SLAs across every public-sector offering ([18], [19]).
  2. Enforce Deterministic OT/SCADA Interlocks: Never place probabilistic AI outputs in unguarded closed-loop physical control over power, water, or transport systems; require independent hardware/software safety limits ([21]).
  3. Wire AI SOC Alerts to NIS2 24h/72h Clocks: Ensure model poisoning, hijacked agent connectors, or grid-AI outages page crisis leadership within NIS2 Article 23 notification windows ([20]).

Key Takeaways #

  1. Vertical regulators do not wait for horizontal AI dates. Financial (DORA, SR 26-2, RBI), clinical (HIPAA, FDA), and labor (BetrVG, NYC LL 144, IL HB 3773) rules block or penalize non-compliant AI immediately.
  2. Fourth-party ICT concentration and vendor lock-in are regulated risks. EU DORA Article 28 and OMB M-25-22 force enterprises and suppliers to map downstream sub-processors and guarantee tested exit portability.
  3. Feature evolution crosses regulatory boundaries silently. Adding automated diagnostic priority to clinical scribes converts CDS into FDA SaMD; enabling employee prompt metrics on enterprise copilots triggers German co-determination vetoes.
  4. Integrate domain QMS instead of duplicating compliance silos. MDCG 2025-6 for EU MDR medical devices and SR 26-2 for banking ERM show regulators expect AI controls embedded into established sector governance.
  5. Physical infrastructure and citizen rights demand deterministic fallbacks. Critical infrastructure operators must backstop AI advisories with non-AI OT safety limits, NIS2 24h incident triage, and guaranteed human review.
